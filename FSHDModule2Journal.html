Module 2: FSHD Clinical Trial Project¶

Project Goal:¶

FSHD is a progressive muscular disease that can cause muscle weakness and degeneration over time. However, because disease progression varies between individuals and can occur gradually, measuring these changes accurately is challenging in clinical trials.

The goal of this project is to analyze FSHD patient data to compare MRI-derived muscle fat fraction and muscle strength measurements to find out which is more sensitive to disease progression. By examining how these measurements change over time, we want to identify which gives us a more consistent and reliable indication of muscle degeneration. Our findings will help us recommend which measurement may be more useful when evaluating new FSHD treatments in clinical trials.

Research Question: Which is more sensitive to FSHD disease progression, MRI fat fraction or muscle strength?

Disease Background:¶

  • Please provide citations and/or hyperlinks to where you obtained information (including AI) for each of the bullet points.

The following 7 bullets seem most relevant, but feel free to choose others if you like:

  • Prevalence & incidence

FSHD is one of the most common muscular dystrophies in adults, affecting roughly 1 in 8,000 to 1 in 20,000 people. In the U.S., about 1 in 15,000 to 20,000 adults have it, and men and women are affected equally (Centro Dino Ferrari, n.d.; MedlinePlus, n.d.-a). A study in the Netherlands found about 52 new diagnoses per year and a prevalence of 12 per 100,000 people. The researchers believe the true number is higher, since many mild cases go undiagnosed. FSHD is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, so a child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of getting it, but 10–30% of cases appear with no family history (Deenen et al., 2014; Preston et al., 2020).

  • Symptoms

As the name suggests, FSHD mainly affects the face, shoulder blades, and upper arms. The weakness is often uneven between the two sides of the body and slowly spreads downward to the trunk and legs. Early signs include trouble closing the eyes, whistling, or making facial expressions. Symptoms usually start between ages 10 and 26, though some carriers never develop any, and 50–80% of patients experience chronic pain. The disease progresses slowly over decades. About 20% of patients eventually need a wheelchair, but life expectancy is usually normal (FSHD Society, n.d.; MedlinePlus, n.d.-a; Preston et al., 2020).

  • Diagnosis

Doctors suspect FSHD from the pattern of weakness and family history, then confirm it with a genetic blood test. Both types involve a region on chromosome 4 called D4Z4, which normally keeps a gene called DUX4 switched off. In FSHD1 (about 95% of cases), this region is shortened. In FSHD2 (about 5%), it loses its normal “silencing,” usually because of a mutation in the SMCHD1 gene. In both cases, DUX4 becomes active and damages muscle cells. Muscle biopsies are now only used when genetic testing is inconclusive (Centro Dino Ferrari, n.d.; MedlinePlus, n.d.-b; Preston et al., 2020).

  • Standard of care treatments (& reimbursement) :

Currently, there are no cures or FDA-approved disease-modifying treatments for FSHD. Rather, standard care focuses on managing the symptoms, maintaining mobility, and improving the quality of life (Preston & Wang, 2025). Physical therapy is highly recommended for those suffering from FSHD, with the intent of establishing appropriate exercise programs, whilst assistive devices like ankle-foot orthoses can improve mobility and reduce the risk of falls in patients experiencing foot drop. Additionally, occupational therapy may help patients experiencing difficulty with daily tasks. In specific cases, surgical fixation of the scapula can improve shoulder movement, and chronic pain can be managed through physical therapy and medication (Preston & Wang, 2025). Reimbursement is more dependent on how much the user's insurance covers and medical necessity. Under Original Medicare, Part B covers medically necessary physical and occupational therapy. Upon meeting the Part B deductible, patients will pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2026). Coverage and out-of-pocket costs for surgical procedures and assistive devices depend on their specific coverage requirement.

  • Disease progression & prognosis :

FSHD is a slowly progressive muscular dystrophy, but the rate and the severity of muscle degeneration vary amongst individuals. Patients can experience periods of relatively stable muscle function followed by periods of rapid deterioration. Disease progression is asymmetric; muscles on the left and right sides can differ in the rate at which they weaken (Preston & Wang, 2025). The age of onset can influence prognosis, with earlier onset cases associated with more severe disease progression. While FSHD progresses, healthy skeletal muscle tissue undergoes atrophy and can be replaced by fatty and fibrotic tissue, which reduces the muscles' ability to generate force. 20% of affected individuals end up requiring wheelchair assistance, but typically, life expectancy is not shortened (Preston & Wang, 2025). Because muscle degeneration occurs gradually and unevenly, it's harder to monitor disease progression. MRI provides a way to evaluate these changes in muscle composition, including fatty infiltration and signals connected with inflammation. The provided measurements help identify underlying changes that may not be immediately reflected in physical performance, making MRI valuable for monitoring disease progression and for evaluating what treatments are needed for clinical trials (Wong et al., 2024).

  • Biological mechanisms (anatomy, organ physiology, cell & molecular physiology) :

FSHD affects skeletal muscles, those responsible for voluntary movement and generating force. Skeletal muscles consist of bundles of muscle fibers that have myofibrils, which are made up of sarcomeres. These sarcomeres have actin and myosin filaments interacting to produce muscle contractions. When FSHD damages muscle fibers, the amount of functional contractile tissue decreases, which reduces the muscle's ability to generate force and perform normal actions (Preston & Wang, 2025). FSHD is caused by the abnormal expression of the DUX4 gene, which is active during early embryonic development, but is silenced within the mature skeletal muscle. There are two primary forms of FSHD: FSHD1, which makes up 95% of cases, results from a contraction of the D4Z4 DNA repeat region on chromosome 4, leading to reduced DNA methylation and inappropriate DUX4 expression. FSHD2, which makes up the remaining 5% of cases, is associated with mutations in the SMCHD1 gene, which disrupts the normal epigenetic silencing of DUX4 (MedlinePlus Genetics, n.d.; Preston & Wang, 2025). DUX4 encodes a transcription factor that influences the expression of other genes. When it is abnormally expressed within skeletal muscle, it will activate gene programs that contribute to cellular dysfunction, inflammation, and muscle damage. Over time, this leads to muscle atrophy and the replacement of functional muscle fibers. But these changes can be observed through MRI, which provides a connection between the molecular cause of FSHD and the changes in muscle composition (Wong et al., 2024).

  • Clinical Trials/next-gen therapies :

The current clinical trials for FSHD involve research into therapies targeting the molecular basis of muscle degradation, mainly the deregulation of DUX4 expression (Wong et al., 2024). An example of a therapy that was studied in the past is Losmapimod, an experimental drug from Fulcrum Therapeutics that was made to regulate DUX4 expression through inhibition of the p38 MAPK pathway. While it was promising in earlier studies, it didn't prove to show a statistically significant improvement in its primary outcome, the Reachable Workspace in its Phase 3 REACH clinical trial and then was discontinued in 2024 (Kinoshita, 2024; Voermans et al., 2026). Other drugs under investigation include del-brax AOC 1020, an experimental RNA-targeted therapeutic meant to lower DUX4 expression, and EPI-321, an epigenetic editing therapeutic aimed at restoring DUX4 silencing (Novartis, 2026; UCLA Health, 2026). Data Set:¶

Describe the FSHD dataset used in your project. Include:

For this project, we used the provided FSHD dataset, which includes information from 34 participants with measurements collected at baseline, Year 1, and Year 2. The clinical dataset has patient information such as age, sex, and D4Z4 repeat length, along with MRI measurements, muscle strength, and functional assessments. Since our research question focuses on finding whether MRI fat fraction or muscle strength is more sensitive to FSHD disease progression, these were the two main measurements used in our analysis. We also used the provided MRI image dataset to find changes in fat content within the tibialis anterior muscle.

  • The subject and visit/timepoint(s) analyzed

We performed two analyses. For the clinical analysis, we used the 21 of 34 participants who had complete right-leg TA fat fraction and dorsiflexion strength at baseline, Year 1, and Year 2. Because visit times were different (Year 1: 11.15–18.18 months; Year 2: 22.13–42.77 months), we used linear interpolation to estimate each participant’s values at 11.15 and 22.13 months, the earliest visit times. That way every estimate falls within the measured data. For the MRI image analysis, we examined eight slices of the left TA from subject UW_0020 at two visits, TP1 and TP2. This subject matches participant 1-0020 in the clinical dataset, whose Year 1 visit was 16.8 months after baseline. This participant had no Year 2 data, so they were not part of the 21-participant comparison. The MRI analysis was used to show how fat fraction is calculated and how it varies along the muscle and between visits.

  • The tibialis anterior MRI data

The tibialis anterior is the muscle at the front of the lower leg that lifts the foot (dorsiflexion). Weakness in this muscle causes foot drop, which is common in FSHD. Wong et al. (2024) imaged and biopsied the TA on both legs of every participant. That makes it the one muscle where MRI, strength, and molecular data are all available.

  • The water-image and fat-image files

The MRI teaching dataset contains water-signal and fat-signal images saved as NumPy .npy files. These come from a Dixon MRI scan, which separates the signal coming from water which is mostly muscle tissue from the signal coming from fat. Each image is 266 × 224 pixels and shows the half of the leg cross-section that contains the TA, along with some surrounding anatomy and background. According to dataset_info.json, each pixel is 1.116 × 1.116 mm and each slice is 4 mm thick.

  • The tibialis anterior segmentation mask

Each slice has a binary mask in which 1 = TA pixel and 0 = everything else. The masks were provided with the dataset; we did not create or edit them. We used the mask to keep only TA pixels when calculating fat fraction, so that background, bone, subcutaneous fat, and other muscles would not affect the result.

  • The sampled locations along the muscle

The MRI dataset had eight sampled locations along the tibialis anterior muscle at each timepoint, corresponding to 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 60%, 70%, and 80% of the way along the muscle. The provided slice_info.csv file identifies each slice's timepoint, percentage location, and distance from the start of the muscle in millimeters. The same percentage location did not always correspond to the same physical distance at both visits. For example, the 40% slice was located at 92 mm at TP1 and 96 mm at TP2. Therefore, we used distance in millimeters rather than percentage location for our interpolation analysis. We calculated fat fraction at the available locations for TP1 and TP2 and used linear interpolation to estimate fat fraction at 100 mm along the muscle. This allowed us to compare fat fraction at the same distance from the muscle start between the two timepoints.

  • The meaning and units of the image signals

The water and fat images contain MRI signal intensities in the scanner’s own arbitrary units, so the raw numbers don’t mean much by themselves. We combined them as fat fraction (%) = fat / (fat + water) × 100. A value near 0% means mostly healthy muscle and a value near 100% means the muscle has mostly been replaced by fat. Outside the leg there is almost no signal, so the ratio there is just noise, which is why we only used pixels inside the TA mask.

  • The clinical, biomarker, or functional measurements included in the dataset

The clinical dataset has 34 participants who were measured at baseline, Year 1, and Year 2, and it records how many months after baseline each visit happened. For each person it lists sex, age, and D4Z4 repeat size. On the MRI side, it gives the whole-muscle fat fraction (%) of the tibialis anterior (TA) on both legs, plus a STIR value that shows inflammation or swelling in the muscle which is only at baseline and Year 1. There is also a DUX4 score from the TA biopsies taken at baseline, which shows how active the genes turned on by DUX4 are. Strength was measured as how hard each foot could pull upward (dorsiflexion), in kilograms. The dataset also has timed tests of function in seconds: the Timed Up-and-Go, a 10-meter walk/run, and going up and down four stairs. Finally, the Clinical Severity Score rates the disease from 0 to 10, with higher scores meaning more severe. We chose TA fat fraction and TA strength for our analysis because they measure the same muscle. We also checked that their ranges in the dataset (3.3–82.2% and 1.8–43 kg) match the ones in Wong et al. (2024).

  • The source of the data

Both datasets were provided through BME 2315 Module 2 and come from the study by Wong et al. (2024). That study enrolled 34 FSHD participants (16 female, 18 male, ages 21–69) at the University of Washington, University of Rochester, and University of Kansas. Participants had lower-leg MRI on 3T Siemens scanners using a Dixon sequence (1.1 × 1.1 × 4 mm voxels), strength testing of the TA in kilograms, and biopsies of both TA muscles. The TA was outlined on every MRI slice by an AI program and then checked by trained staff, and whole-muscle fat fraction was calculated as the average pixel fat fraction across the whole TA. According to the README, the MRI teaching files are cross-sections of the left TA of subject UW_0020 at two visits, each with a water image, a fat image, and a mask. The masks were provided, so we did not create them ourselves.

Reference: Wong, C.-J., et al. (2024). Regional and bilateral MRI and gene signatures in facioscapulohumeral dystrophy: Implications for clinical trial design and mechanisms of disease progression. Human Molecular Genetics, 33(8), 698–708. https://doi.org/10.1093/hmg/ddae007 Additional data source: BME 2315 Module 2, provided FSHD clinical dataset and MRI teaching dataset, slice_info.csv.

muscle = ff[mask & valid]

print("TA standard deviation:", round(np.std(muscle), 2))

Interpolation Analysis:¶

For this part of our Interpolation Analysis, we used linear interpolation to estimate the fat fraction at a location along the tibialis anterior (TA) muslce where we did not have a direct sampled MRI slice. From the data set, we used the distance_from_muscle_start_mm column from slice_info.csv to identify each of the slice's location in mm. Specifically, for subject UW_0020, we analysed eight of the sampled location at both TP1 and TP2, and calculated the average fat fraction within the TA muslce, respective to it's location.

We chose to estimate the fat fraction at 100mm along the muscle using np.interp(). Since 100mm fell between two measured location at both visits, we performed interpolation rather than extrapolation. At TP1, the surrounding measured locations were 92mm and 116mm, and at TP2, they were 96mm and 120mm. Linear interpolation assumes that the fat fraction changes at a constant rate between the neighboring measurements.

Through this method, we estimated a fat fraction of 26.91% at TP1 and 52.40% at TP2 at 100mm. Our graph shows the measured mean fat fractions along the muscle, with diamond shaped markers representing the interpolated estimates. The results suggest that fat fraction increased between the two visits at this location, indicating increased fat replacement within the muscle.

But, these interpolated values are estimates rather than new MRI measurements. The actual fat fraction at 100mm can differ because fat may not have been distributed evenly between the sampled locations. Additionally, differences in image quality, the muscle segmentation, and the limited number of sampled slices can affect the accuracy of our estimates.

#Select the patients with complete MRI and QMT measurements
time_cols = ["lag_months_baseline", "lag_months_y1", "lag_months_y2"]

ff_cols = ["ff_baseline_R", "ff_y1_R", "ff_y2_R"]

qmt_cols = [
    "dorsiflexion_qmt_R_baseline",
    "dorsiflexion_qmt_R_y1",
    "dorsiflexion_qmt_R_y2"
]

complete = df.dropna(subset=time_cols + ff_cols + qmt_cols).copy()

#Function for linear interpolation
def interpolate_patient(row, columns, target):
    times = [row[col] for col in time_cols]
    values = [row[col] for col in columns]

    f = interp1d(times, values, kind="linear")
    return float(f(target))

#Interpolate both measurements at T1 and T2
for name, columns in [("FF", ff_cols), ("QMT", qmt_cols)]:

        lambda row: interpolate_patient(row, columns, T1), axis=1

        lambda row: interpolate_patient(row, columns, T2), axis=1

def quadratic_change(row, columns):
    times = [row[col] for col in time_cols]
    values = [row[col] for col in columns]

    f = interp1d(times, values, kind="quadratic")

    value_T1 = float(f(T1))
    value_T2 = float(f(T2))

    return (value_T2 - value_T1) / value_T1 * 100

Conclusions:¶

Explain what your analysis suggests about measuring FSHD progression.

Based on our research question, “Which is more sensitive to FSHD disease progression, MRI fat fraction or muscle strength?”, our analysis suggests that MRI fat fraction may be a more sensitive measure of disease progression than muscle strength in the dataset we examined.

Discuss:

  • Which MRI, biomarker, or functional measures changed most consistently

MRI fat fraction showed more consistent evidence of change compared to muscle strength. The average MRI fat fraction increased by 5.88%, with a standard deviation of 12.38, while muscle strength increased by 4.98%, with a higher standard deviation of 20.24. MRI fat fraction also showed a statistically significant change (p = 0.0417), while muscle strength did not (p = 0.2732). These results suggest that MRI fat fraction may be better at detecting gradual changes in muscle composition, although the statistical results alone do not prove that it is more sensitive.

  • Which measures appeared most sensitive to differences across muscle locations or visits

MRI fat fraction seems to be more sensitive to changes between visits and across muscle locations. In our analysis of the tibialis anterior muscle, the interpolated fat fraction at 100 mm increased from 26.91% at TP1 to 52.40% at TP2. This shows an increase in fatty infiltration at that location between visits. MRI also allowed us to find differences across slices of the muscle, which muscle strength testing cannot directly measure. However, these MRI results were based on one subject and should not be generalized to all patients.

  • Whether the measures could be useful as biomarkers or exploratory clinical-trial endpoints

Our findings suggest that MRI fat fraction could be useful in FSHD clinical trials because it helps researchers measure changes in muscle fat over time. MRI may detect disease progression even when changes in muscle strength are small or hard to notice. However, muscle strength testing is also important because it shows how well patients can use their muscles in everyday activities. Using both methods together could help researchers better understand how FSHD progresses.

  • What additional evidence would be needed before using them to evaluate treatment effectiveness Before MRI fat fraction or muscle strength could be used to evaluate treatment effectiveness, more research would be needed with larger patient populations and longer follow-up periods. Researchers would also need to confirm that the measurements are reliable and reproducible across different patients, muscle groups, and testing conditions. Additionally, studies should investigate whether changes in MRI fat fraction are associated with meaningful changes in muscle strength and patients' daily functioning. Treatment studies would be necessary to establish whether these measurements can reliably detect the effects of therapy.

  • Potential ethical implications of selecting one measure over another, including patient relevance, accessibility, and the risk of overlooking meaningful changes in function

Choosing one measurement over another raises ethical concerns related to patient accessibility, cost, and clinical relevance. MRI provides detailed information about muscle composition, but it can be expensive and may not be easily accessible to all patients. Muscle strength testing is generally more accessible and directly measures physical abilities that affect patients' daily lives. Relying only on MRI could overlook important functional changes, while relying only on muscle strength could miss early changes in muscle composition. Using both methods together could help doctors better understand how FSHD progresses and how it affects patients.

Limitations and Future Work:¶

Describe the limitations of your project, including limitations of the sample size, available visits, MRI measurements, segmentation, biomarkers, functional measures, and analysis methods.

If you had more time or additional data, explain how you would improve the analysis. Examples include analyzing more participants, adding more timepoints, improving segmentation, comparing additional muscles, validating the MRI measure against functional outcomes, or testing whether the measure is sensitive to treatment response.

A major limitation of our study was having a small sample size. Although the clinical dataset included 34 participants with FSHD, only 21 had the complete MRI fat fraction and muscle strength measurements needed for our analysis. This limits how well our findings shows the larger FSHD population. Additionally, participants were followed for approximately two years with only three visits, which may not be enough to observe the full progression of a FSHD that often grows and progresses slowly over time. Another limitation was that our clinical analysis focused only on the right tibialis anterior muscle, while our detailed MRI image analysis examined the left tibialis anterior muscle of a single patient, UW_0020, at two timepoints. Since FSHD can affect muscles differently and may progress unevenly between the two sides of the body, our results may not apply to other muscles or patients, and we can’t speak for other muscles that are affected by this disease. Our detailed MRI analysis also included only eight slices per visit and relied on the provided segmentation masks. Errors in identifying muscle boundaries or differences between slices could affect the calculated fat fraction values. Furthermore, our analysis focused only on MRI fat fraction and muscle strength, so it did not include other biomarkers or functional measurements that could provide additional information about disease progression. For future work, we would include more patients, so we can have more time to follow them over for a longer period with additional visits to better understand how FSHD progresses. We would also examine additional muscle groups to determine whether the same patterns occur throughout the body. In the future studies, we would also compare MRI fat fraction and muscle strength with other biomarkers and functional measurements to determine which methods best show the disease progression. Finally, studying patients before and after treatment could help researchers determine whether MRI fat fraction or muscle strength is more useful for detecting treatment-related changes in FSHD clinical trials.
